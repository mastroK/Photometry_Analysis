Summary

No robust evidence of an age difference in RPE encoding

Five metrics from the within-animal RPE analyses were compared between the 7 FP1 mice and 4 FP2 mice with a Mann-Whitney U test, each run twice: once across all 11 mice, and once excluding WCL25 and WCL26 — the two FP1 mice independently flagged in the pooled report as low-fit outliers with the fewest trials in the whole dataset, on the hypothesis that they may not have fully learned the task yet. Only one of five metrics (encoding-GLM peak R²) reached conventional significance in the full 11-mouse comparison, and that result did not survive the exclusion check — it is very likely driven by those same two low-fit mice rather than a genuine cohort/age effect. The other four metrics (βRPE, the outcome×value interaction, FIR out-of-sample R², and the signed-vs-unsigned RPE comparison) showed no significant difference in either version.

Bottom line. With n=7 vs n=4, this comparison cannot rule out a real age effect — it simply doesn't find one that survives a basic robustness check. The core RPE-related encoding (value modulating the outcome response, specifically after the event) looks the same in both cohorts; what differs most between them is how well two particular young mice's data fit the models, which has an independent, already-flagged explanation.

Why this can only be a cohort comparison

Age and cohort are perfectly confounded here

Every FP1 mouse was 51–80 days old at recording; every FP2 mouse was 147–167 days old. There is no mouse in between, and no mouse that switches cohorts. That means age cannot be statistically separated from anything else that differs between these two specific groups — different rig/channel era, different training history, different point in the project timeline. Per the plan agreed before running this: viral construct, injection protocol, and sex ratio are confirmed identical across both cohorts, which rules out those specific confounds. The remaining candidates — trial-count balance, hemisphere composition, and task performance — are audited below rather than assumed away.

Interpretation

What this does and doesn't establish

This analysis does not find a robust age-related difference in mPFC reward/value encoding between young and old mice. The one metric that looked different (encoding-model peak R²) stops looking different once the two mice already suspected of incomplete task learning are set aside — the more parsimonious explanation is that WCL25/26's low trial counts and poor fits are a training/data-amount issue specific to those two animals, not a cohort-wide age signature. Everything else — the size and sign of the value-modulation effect itself, and how well the FIR model captures it — looks statistically indistinguishable between 7-to-11-week-old and 21-to-24-week-old mice in this dataset.

This is a modest result, not a strong negative: with only 4 FP2 mice, this comparison has limited power to detect anything short of a large effect. It rules out a large, cohort-wide age effect in the direction and metrics tested here; it does not rule out a smaller one, and it cannot separate "age" from the other things that differ between these two specific groups of animals.

Scope and what would settle this more definitively. Age and cohort are fully confounded in this dataset — any difference found here (or not found) is a property of these two specific groups of mice, not a controlled age manipulation. The planned analysis of FP1_retrained_processed_data (the same FP1 mice, recorded again later, older) is a genuine within-subject design: it would let each animal serve as its own control across age, which this between-cohort comparison cannot do, and is the stronger test of a true age effect once available.
